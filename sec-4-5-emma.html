    Section 3.5 Emma’s Section

    Objectives: Section 3.5 Emma’s Section

    Students will be able to:
    🔗
    • Describe and interpret the measures of center: mean and median.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Analyze the relationship of the mean and median to the shape of the data.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Determine whether the mean or median is a more accurate measure of centrality in various contexts.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify the strenghts and limitations of the mean and median as measures of centrality.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Calculating Summary Statistics

    In addition to graphical and verbal descriptions, we can use numbers to summarize quantitative distributions. We want to know what an “average” value is (where the data is centered), and how spread out the values are. Together, the center and spread provide important information which can be used to estimate our population parameters. In this section we will discuss the measures of center and in the next section we will discuss the measures of spread.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Measures of Center

    There are a few different types of “averages” that measure the center, and the one we use will depend on the shape of the distribution. We will focus on the two most common “averages”: the mean and the median.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Mean

    The mean, or more formally the arithmetic mean, is what probably comes to mind when you hear the word average. The mean is a single number which represents a “typical” value of a data set. The calculation of the mean uses every data value in the distribution and is therefore strongly affected by skew and outliers.
    🔗
    To calculate the mean of a distribution, we divide the sum of the data values by the number of data values we have.
    🔗

    Subsection Median

    Example 3.5.2.

    In 2024, the median household income in the United States was calculated to be $83,730.
     19 
    Source: Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey, 2024 and 2025 Annual Social and Economic Supplements (CPS ASEC; DMS number P-7534374, DRB approval number CBDRB-FY25-0384).
    🔗
    🔗
    In order to properly interpret the meaning of the example above, we want to be sure to understand what the median is. The median of a data set is the “middle” value, when the data are listed in order from smallest to largest. We can also think of the median as the value that has 50% of the data below it and 50% of data above it. As we will discover later, the median is also the 50th percentile.
    🔗
    In the context of our example, then, $83,730 is the center of median household incomes in the United States. Around 50% of households in the United States have an income below $83,730 and the other 50% have an income above $83,730.
    🔗
    For both mean and median, it is important to not overgeneralize the findings. If the mean or median is found from a small sample size, the value might not apply to the larger population. Consider if you were to survey ten households in your neighborhood about their income. This median might provide a good estimate for what the typical household income in your neighborhood is; however, this would not necessarily be a good estimate for the typical income in the United States. You would likely find a median quite different from the $83,730 reported in the example above.
    🔗

    Example 3.5.3.

    A study was conducted on a random group of 25 students from a single high school. The researchers found the students to have an average daily screen time of 7 hours. Is it reasonable to say that typical people have a daily screen time of around 7 hours? What population can this average be generalized to?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Solely based on the study conducted, we cannot say the amount of time a typical person spends on a screen is 7 hours. This study was conducted on students aged 14-18. We do not have data on other age ranges. Additionally, this study took place at one high school. The average could only be generalized to students at that specific high school.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    🔗
    Michael Jordan’s income in the activity above is what we call an outlier. The mean is easily impacted by outliers because we use each of the data points to calculate the mean. Extremely high or low values will greatly affect the mean. The median, however, is relatively unchanged by outliers because it is the “middle” data point in a data set. It will generally give a more accurate measure of the center of the data.
    🔗
    For this reason, when working with data that is has outliers, it is common to use the median.
    🔗
    🔗

    The shape of the distribution is unimodal and roughly symmetric. We can also see from the statistics output that the mean and the median are approximately equal. inches inches
    🔗
    🔗
    As shown in the three examples shown above, If a distribution is skewed, the mean is pulled in the direction of the skew. In a right skewed distribution, the mean is greater than the median, while in a left skewed distribution, the mean is less than the median. If the distribution is symmetric, the mean and the median will be approximately equal.
    🔗
    We can use these observations in reverse as well. If we know the mean is greater than the median, then we can expect the distribution to be skewed to the right. If the mean is less than the median, then we can expect the distribution to be skewed to the left. When the mean and the median are approximately equal, the distribution is likely to be symmetric.
    🔗

    Example 3.5.10.

    Recent college graduates were asked how much student loan debt they have. The data has a mean of $46,265 and a median of $33,652. Just based on this information, do you expect the distribution to be symmetric, skewed to the left, or skewed to the right?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Since the mean is greater than the median, we can expect the distribution to be skewed to the right.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    As mentioned earlier, when dealing with data that is skewed or has outliers, it is common to use the median instead of the mean. In these instances, the median will give a better measure of what is "typical." When the data is roughly symmetric, the mean and median will be approximately equal, and either measure could be used.
    🔗

You find a list of sale prices for that make and model, and you see that the distribution of those prices is skewed to the right. Some of the prices at the high end are close to the original sale price of the car, so you guess that those cars might have really low mileage, or have other enhancements added on that increased the value (but which don’t apply to the car you found).
      🔗
      🔗
    2. You are asked to find the average height of a large population of people. The distribution of heights follows a symmetric, normal distribution.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. You are trying to determine how much a “typical” house costs in an area. There are a small number of houses which are incredibly valuable in the area.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Customers are asked to take a satisfaction survey on their experiences in a store. The rating follows a 1-5 scale with 1 being extremely dissatisfied and 5 being extremely satisfied. You want to find the typical level of customer satisfaction.
      🔗

    Subsection Limitations of Measures of Center

    While measures of center are useful in identifying a typical value, relying on only measures of centrality can often hide important characteristics of the data. The following example is one such example of this.
    🔗
    Activity
    In 1950, the Air Force attempted to measure particular bodily dimensions of a group of pilots. The goal was to find an average pilot size and use these average dimensions in the design of cockpits. Lieutenant Gilbert S. Daniels measured over 4,000 pilots and challenged the idea of finding an "average man." Use the link to read about Daniel’s findings and argument to answer the following questions. The "Average Man"?
     20 
    apps.dtic.mil/sti/tr/pdf/AD0010203.pdf
    🔗
    1. Looking at the first two pages of Daniels’ argument, what are some issues associated with trying to find the "average man"?
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Of the 4,063 pilots that were measured, how many were approximately average for all 10 of the physical dimensions measured?
      🔗
      🔗
    3. Lieutenant Daniels suggests looking at a range of "average" for more accurate data. Looking at Table I in the document, how did the use of a range of "average" affect the data?
      🔗
      🔗
    4. How did Lieutenant Daniels use math to make a well formed argument?
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    To understand a dataset as a whole, there is value in considering both the average and the variability of the data. With our example of the "Average Man" for instance, a range of common body dimensions would allow for better generalizations and design decisions. Indeed, Lieutenant Daniels’ discovery led to the use of adjustable seating and straps to fit the majority of pilots rather than a "one size fits all" approach.
    🔗
    The mean or median of a dataset can be valuable information, but it does not necessarily provide us information about how spread out the data is. Consider, for instance, our earlier example on household income in the United States. The median was $83,730. Is it reasonable for us to say that most households will have an income close to $83,730? We cannot necessarily jump to that conclusion without taking a closer look at the distribution of the data. Measures of variation will be covered in the next section, and they can help shed some light on the spread and consistency of a dataset.
    🔗
    🔗
