    Section 3.1 Overview of the Statistical Process

    Objectives: Section 3.1 Overview of the Statistical Process

    Students will be able to:
    🔗
    • Identify the main research question of a study or experiment.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Define and identify the population, parameter, sample, and statistic.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify four sampling methods: simple random, stratified, systematic, and convenience sampling.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify and discuss types of bias associated with sampling.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Distinguish between observational studies and experiments.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Evaluate the generalizability of a study or experiment.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Interpret the margin of error and confidence interval within the context of a study or experiement.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Introduction to Statistics.

    We are bombarded by statistics every day. From billboards to news headlines to social media posts, statistical claims are just about everywhere we look. Consider the following examples:
    • 4 out of 5 dentists recommend Dentyne.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Almost 85% of lung cancers in men and 45% in women are tobacco-related.
      🔗
      🔗
    • People tend to be more persuasive when they look others directly in the eye and speak loudly and quickly.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Women make 75 cents to every dollar a man makes when they work the same job.
      🔗
      🔗
    • A surprising new study shows that eating egg whites can increase one’s life span.
      🔗
      🔗
    • It is highly unlikely there will ever be another baseball player with a batting average over 400.
      🔗
      🔗
    • There is an 80% chance that in a room full of 30 people, at least two people will share a birthday.
      🔗
      🔗
    • 79.48% of all statistics are made up on the spot.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    Some of these claims may sound familiar; if not, you have likely heard similar ones. Evaluating claims such as these can feel daunting, especially if they are meant to inform significant decisions. However, you do not need to be a professional statistician to interpret and apply statistics wisely. With some key background in the discipline, you will be well-equipped to become a thoughtful consumer of statistics.
    🔗

    Subsection Case Studies

    Let’s begin with the following two case studies:
    🔗
    Case Study 1: The National Opinion Research Center administers the General Social Survey every two years to collect data on demographics, education, work, and other characteristics of U.S. residents. In 2010, NORC randomly selected 1,172 individuals from across the country to participate in this survey. Suppose a NORC employee used the GSS data to calculate the average number of hours worked per week across different levels of education.
     1 
    National Opinion Research Center, General Social Survey, 2010
    🔗
    Case Study 2: A migraine is a particularly painful type of headache, which patients sometimes wish to treat with acupuncture. To determine whether acupuncture relieves migraine pain, researchers conducted a randomized controlled study where 89 females, ages 15-60, diagnosed with migraine headaches were randomly assigned to one of two groups. The first group consisted of 43 patients who received acupuncture specifically designed to treat migraines. The second group consisted of 46 patients who received a form of fake acupuncture (needle insertion at non-acupoint locations). Twenty-four hours after patients received acupuncture, they were asked if they were pain-free. Results are summarized in the contingency table below.
     2 
    G. Allais et al. “Ear acupuncture in the treatment of migraine attacks: a randomized trial on the efficacy of appropriate versus inappropriate acupoints”. In: Neurological Sci. 32.1 (2011), pp. 173–175. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21533739/
    🔗
    Although brief, these case studies offer great insight into the statistical process. That is, the process researchers and statisticians use to formulate statistical claims. Thoughtful consumers of statistics are also familiar with this process and use their knowledge to ask critical questions about the information they encounter. Therefore, the goal of this section is to introduce you to the statistical process and some key questions that will help you understand, evaluate, and apply the claims you come across in everyday life.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Statistical Process

    The following diagram outlines the statistical process and some relevant considerations for consumers.
    🔗
    The green terms will serve as the foci of this section. As we explore them within the context of our case studies and other relevant examples, you will gain a better understanding of how to answer the questions above.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Research Question

    Every statistical exploration begins with a question or something the researcher is curious about. As a consumer of statistics, it is crucial to identify the main research question so that you understand the purpose of the study or experiment. In our second case study, for example, it is evident that the researchers intended to answer the question, “Does acupuncture relieve migraine pain?”.
    🔗
    Sometimes, the main research question is not as obvious. This is often the case when we retrieve statistical information from secondary sources like news broadcasts. In our first case study, we saw that the NORC employee calculated the average number of hours worked per week for people of different education levels. Why was this the case, though? Did she simply want to know how many hours people from each demographic typically work? Or did she intend to compare these averages and explore a possible association between the two variables? In this sense, you may need to do some extra digging to identify the main research question. We suggest finding the original report of the study or experiment, as researchers often articulate their intentions in the report’s abstract.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.1.

    It is also a good practice to consider the extent to which the given data can answer the researcher’s main question. Let’s imagine that an educational research team wanted to determine if a new instructional strategy enhanced students’ understanding of a particular math topic.
    🔗
    To answer this question, the team decided to analyze the test scores of two groups of students. The first group was in a classroom where the new instructional strategy was implemented. The second was in a classroom where the traditional strategy was used.
    🔗
    Thoughtful consumers of statistics recognize that while the test scores may provide some insight into the strategy’s effectiveness, they do not paint a complete picture of students’ understanding. Further, other factors such as test-taking skills, home environment, motivation, and stress can impact student performance. In this sense, test scores alone may not be able to answer the team’s research question.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Population

    After identifying the main research question, consumers must consider the population of interest. That is, the group the researchers wanted to learn about. Note that the term “population” does not apply solely to people groups. It can describe groups of other living things, such as tomato plants, and even groups of non-living things, like auto parts.
    🔗
    The population of interest is also known as the target population. In some cases, participants in a poorly designed study or experiment fail to represent the intended population. Therefore, the target population may differ from the real population.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.2.

    Think for a moment about our two case studies. What populations did the researchers aim to learn about through these projects?
    🔗
    Solution.
    In the first case study, we see that the purpose of the GSS is to collect information about U.S. residents. However, it is important to note that since the survey includes questions about educational attainment and weekly work hours, it is intended for people 18 and over. Therefore, the study’s target population is U.S. adult residents.
    🔗
    In the acupuncture experiment, we see that the people who participated were females diagnosed with migraine headaches. Assuming this group was a representative sample (we will explore this term momentarily), the population of interest is females who suffer from migraines.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Keep in mind, populations do not have to contain millions of people or units of study. Grace College’s student body of 1,400 students, for example, could serve as a population for a survey.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Parameter

    A parameter is a numerical value, such as a percentage or average, that describes a population. Parameters are usually theoretical quantities that researchers attempt to estimate since it is often expensive, time-consuming, or impossible to collect data from the entire population.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.3.

    Identify one parameter from the GSS case study that the NORC employee attempted to estimate.
    🔗
    Solution.
    The NORC employee estimated the average number of hours U.S. residents with bachelor’s degrees work.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Sample

    To estimate a parameter, researchers select a smaller subset of the entire population called a sample. When you come across a study or experiment, you will want to determine whether the researchers chose a representative sample. That is, one that reflects the characteristics of the population.
    🔗
    The importance of selecting a representative sample cannot be understated. If researchers successfully do so, the results of their study or experiment are said to be generalizable, or applicable to most of the target population.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.4.

    Suppose your friend, Sean, makes the following comment after reading the acupuncture case study: “Wow, it sounds like acupuncture might have the potential to help all people who suffer from migraines! I’ll recommend it to my brother, Tom. His migraines have been pretty severe lately.” Is Sean’s statement supported by the study?
    🔗
    Solution.
    No. Because the experiment was only performed on females, it is not representative of all people who suffer from migraines. Moreover, it may not be wise for Sean to recommend acupuncture to his brother without consulting some additional sources.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Sampling Methods

    Researchers often obtain representative samples using randomness. Consider the following four types of sampling, three of which have an element of randomness.
    🔗
    Simple Random Sampling (SRS) – a type of random sampling in which every member of the population has an equal probability of being chosen
    🔗
    Stratified Sampling – a type of sampling in which a population is divided into subgroups or strata, and then random samples are taken from each subgroup. The sample sizes are often proportional to the size of each subgroup in the population.
    🔗
    Systematic Sampling – a type of random sampling in which every \(n^{th}\) member of the population is selected to be in the sample. The starting position is often chosen at random.
    🔗
    Convenience Sampling – a type of sampling in which participants are selected based on whoever is convenient. Generally speaking, this the worst type of sampling because it does not have an element of randomness.
    🔗
    Keep in mind that even random samples may not be completely representative of the population due to sampling variation. For example, if a polling organization repeatedly takes samples of 1,000 people from the population of likely voters in the state of Indiana, some of these samples may have a slightly higher percentage of Republicans than the general population, while others may have a higher percentage of Democrats.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.5.

    NORC divides the US into small geographic regions and randomly selects individuals from a representative subset of these regions to participate in the GSS. What type of sampling method is this? What is the benefit of using this method for the GSS?
    🔗
    Solution.
    NORC uses stratified sampling to select the GSS respondents. This method is beneficial because it ensures that residents of diverse geographic regions are represented. Had NORC used simple random sampling or systematic sampling, residents of rural and other sparsely populated areas may have been disproportionately excluded.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Statistical Bias

    Thoughtful consumers of statistics recognize when bias has jeopardized the credibility or generalizability of a study or experiment. Sources of bias can be conscious or unconscious. Consider the following examples:
    🔗
    Sampling Bias – when the sample is not representative of the population
    🔗
    Voluntary Response Bias – the sampling bias that often occurs when the sample is made up of volunteers
    🔗
    Non-Response Bias – when people refuse to participate in a study or drop out of an experiment, we can no longer be certain that our sample is representative of the population
    🔗
    Response Bias – when the responder gives inaccurate responses for any reason
    🔗
    Perceived Lack of Anonymity – when the responder fears giving an honest answer might negatively affect them
    🔗
    Self-Interest Study – bias that can occur when the researchers have an interest in the outcome
    🔗
    Loaded Questions – when the question wording influences the responses
    🔗

    Example 3.1.6.

    1. Consider a study which found that chewing gum may raise math grades in teenagers
       3 
      Reuters. http://news.yahoo.com/s/nm/20090423/od_uk_nm/oukoe_uk_gum_learning. Retrieved 4/27/09
      . This study was conducted by the Wrigley Science Institute, a branch of the Wrigley chewing gum company. This is an example of a self-interest study, one in which the researches have a vested interest in the outcome of the study. While this does not necessarily mean the study was biased, we should subject the study to extra scrutiny.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Consider online reviews of products and businesses. Customers tend to leave reviews if they are very satisfied or very dissatisfied. While you can look for overall patterns and get useful information, these reviews suffer from voluntary response bias and likely capture more extreme views.
      🔗
      🔗
    3. A survey asks participants a question about their interactions with people of different ethnicities. This study could suffer from response bias. A respondent might give an untruthful answer to not be perceived as racist.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. An employer puts out a survey asking their employees if they have a drug abuse problem and need help. In this scenario, answering truthfully might have serious consequences; responses might not be accurate if there is a perceived lack of anonymity and employees fear retribution.
      🔗
      🔗
    5. A survey asks, “Do you support funding research on alternative energy sources to reduce our reliance on high-polluting fossil fuels?” This is an example of a loaded or leading question, a question whose wording leads the respondent towards a certain answer.
      🔗
      🔗
    6. A poll was conducted by phone with the question, “Do you often have time to relax and read a book?” Fifty percent of the people who were called refused to participate in the survey (Probably because they didn’t have the time). It is unlikely that the results will be representative of the entire population. This is an example of non-response bias.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Observational Studies

    Once researchers have selected a representative sample and limited other sources of bias, they can move forward with data collection. There are two main types of data collection: observational studies and experiments.
    🔗
    The GSS case study is an example of an observational study. Researchers perform observational studies when they collect data in ways that do not directly interfere with how the data arise. For instance, researchers may collect information via surveys like the GSS, review medical or company records, or follow a cohort of many similar individuals over time. In each of these situations, researchers merely observe what happens.
    🔗
    In general, observational studies can provide evidence of a naturally occurring association between variables, but they cannot by themselves illustrate a causal connection.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.7.

    After sending the NORC employee’s findings to your friend Alicia, she jokingly responds, “It’s a bummer I didn’t pursue a master’s degree. I would be working a lot less than I am now!” Humor aside, is Alicia’s statement supported by the observational study?
    🔗
    Solution.
    No. While it is true that people with graduate degrees, on average, work fewer hours than those with only bachelor’s degrees, we cannot say that obtaining a master’s degree causes one to work less.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Experiments

    The acupuncture case study is an example of an experiment. In an experiment, a treatment is applied to the subjects, and the results are measured and recorded. Unlike observational studies, experiments can be used to investigate the possibility of a causal connection.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Control Group

    In experiments, the participants are typically divided into a treatment group and a control group. The treatment group receives the treatment being tested; the control group does not.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.8.

    Researchers try to make the conditions for the treatment and control groups as similar as possible. Why do you think they do this?
    🔗
    Solution.
    By creating similar conditions, researchers can isolate the treatment as the only potential source of difference between the groups. A failure to do so can result in confounding. Confounding occurs when there are two or more potential variables that could have caused the outcome, and it is not possible to determine which one actually caused the result.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    Some researchers attempt to ensure that the groups have similar characteristics (e.g., splitting males and females equally between the two groups), but it is nearly impossible to control for every characteristic. For this reason, researchers often use random assignment to create groups.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Placebo-Controlled Experiments

    Sometimes, the mere inclusion of a control group does not eliminate all confounding variables. For example, suppose the control group in our second case study had not received a form of fake acupuncture. If those who received the real acupuncture treatment had shown improvement, the researchers would not have known whether this improvement was due to the acupuncture itself or a misguided belief in the acupuncture’s effectiveness. This scenario introduces a phenomenon known as the placebo effect.
    🔗
    The placebo effect occurs when the effectiveness of a treatment is influenced by the patient’s perception of how effective they think the treatment will be. Thus, a result might be seen even if the treatment is ineffectual.
    🔗
    To control for the placebo effect, a placebo, or dummy treatment, is often given to the control group. This way, both groups can experience the effect of receiving something.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.9.

    It is often more appropriate to administer a conventional treatment rather than a placebo. Why do you suppose this is? Provide an example to support your answer.
    🔗
    Solution.
    In some cases (e.g., a cancer treatment experiment), it would be unethical to administer a placebo or deny any treatment to the control group.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Blind studies

    A blind study includes a placebo; however, the participants do not know whether they are receiving the treatment or a placebo. A double-blind study is one in which the subjects and those interacting with them do not know who is in the treatment group and who is in the control group.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.10.

    In a study about anti-depression medication, for example, you would not want the psychological evaluator to know whether a patient is in the treatment or control group, as it might influence their evaluation. The experiment should be conducted as a double-blind study.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Statistic

    After conducting an observational study or experiment, researchers analyze their collected data and calculate a statistic, such as a percentage or average. The statistic is the researcher’s estimation of the parameter, and it is often the value that consumers are most interested in.
    🔗
    Remember, however, that statistics only prove credible and useful if researchers carry out the statistical process with wisdom and integrity. Further, publication and media sources have a responsibility to communicate researchers’ findings accurately and clearly. In this sense, it is a wise practice to perform additional research on any statistic of interest to ensure that both researchers and the media have upheld these ethical responsibilities.
    🔗

    Example 3.1.11.

    What percentage of the individuals in the treatment group were pain-free after receiving acupuncture?
    🔗
    Solution.
    Approximately 23% of the individuals in the treatment group were pain-free.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Margin of Error and Confidence Intervals

    Even when a study or experiment has successfully avoided bias, there is still an element of variation. To account for this natural variation, researchers publish their margin of error or a confidence interval for their statistics. These numbers describe the precision of the estimate at a certain confidence level.
    🔗
    You’ve probably heard something like, “The candidate has 54% of the likely voters, plus or minus 3%.” The 3% is called the margin of error, so the true percentage (i.e. parameter) is somewhere between 51% and 57%, with a certain level of confidence. To write this as a confidence interval, we place the numbers in parentheses from smallest to largest and separate them with a comma: (51%, 57%).
    🔗
    The most common confidence level is 95%, which means if the poll was conducted repeatedly, and we made a confidence interval each time, we would expect the parameter to fall within our confidence interval 95 out of 100 times. You can learn more on how to calculate the margin of error for different confidence levels in a statistics class.
    🔗
    🔗
