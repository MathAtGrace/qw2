    Section 3.6 Chloe’s Section

    Objectives: Section 3.6 Chloe’s Section

    Students will be able to:
    🔗
    • Define, calculate, and interpret measures of variation, including range, interquartile range, and standard deviation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Identify the strengths and limitations of each measure of variation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Compare distributions using side-by-side boxplots and percentiles.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Calculate and use Z-scores to determine how typical or unusual data values are.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Measures of Variation

    There is an old saying, “Don’t wade across a stream with an average depth of two feet.” Averages and other measures of center are important, but equally important is information about the spread or variability in sets of numbers.
    🔗

    Example 3.6.1.

    Nutrients stored in snags (dead trees still standing) and logs (dead trees that have fallen) are vital to the health of forest ecosystems. In 1991, scientists from the H.J. Andrews Long-Term Ecological Research Center measured the amount of nutrients in snags and logs in a sample forest. In one plot of the forest, the lengths of ten ponderosa pine snags and logs were measured.
     24 
    H.J. Andrews Long Term Ecological Research Center, Log and Snag Dimensions, by Mark Harmon (PI), TD12, plot 1, 1991, http://www.fsl.orst.edu/lter/.
    The mean length was found to be 13.61 meters.
    🔗
    Consider the lengths of the ten trees from the studied plot. Which values seem unusual given the mean? What information does this data set provide that the mean does not capture?
    🔗
    Solution.
    The trees with lengths of 3.0 and 31.0 meters are the most unexpected values given the mean of 13.61 meters. Without seeing the actual data set, we would not know that only one of the data values is relatively close to the mean. In general, the spread of the data is quite large.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Range

    You may already be familiar with the first measure of variation we will discuss in this section: range. The range is the difference between the maximum and minimum values in a data set. While the range provides insight into the scope of the data, it has some significant limitations.
    🔗

    Range.

    \begin{equation*} Range = Max - Min \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.6.3.

    Dungeness crabs are one of the most important commercial species of crab harvested in the eastern Pacific Ocean. Due to their popularity, size limits are used as the primary conservation measure to sustain the crab population in British Columbia, Canada. For example, only Dungeness males with shell widths exceeding 165 mm can be kept.
    🔗
    The following table lists crab boat and harvest data in British Columbia for the years 1980 to 1999.
     25 
    Fisheries and Oceans Canada, Pacific Region, “Stats on 2000 Crab Fishery,” http://www.pac.dfo-mpo.gc.ca/ops/fm/shellfish/crab/default_e.htm.
    🔗
    1. What is the range of the harvest data?
      🔗
    2. What information about the spread of the harvest data is not captured by the range?
      🔗
    3. Make a generalization about the effect of outliers on the range of a data set.
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. The range is \(6,289 - 957 = 5,332\) metric tons.
      🔗
    2. Unfortunately, the range does not provide any information about how the data is distributed. We are unable to tell, for example, that over half of the harvest data falls below 2,000 metric tons. Looking at the data in a histogram, it is evident that the distribution is right-skewed.
      🔗
    3. Outliers significantly impact the range of a data set, as they are often the minimum and maximum values. The presence of outliers can lead us to believe that the data is more “spread out” than it actually is.

    Subsection Interquartile Range

    Perhaps a more insightful measure of variation is the interquartile range (IQR). The IQR describes the spread of the middle 50% of data values.
    🔗
    The IQR is calculated as the difference between the third quartile and the first quartile. As the name implies, quartiles are values that divide the data into quarters. The first quartile (Q1) is the value that 25% of the data lie below. The third quartile (Q3) is the value that 75% of the data lie below.
    🔗

    Interquartile Range (IQR).

    \begin{equation*} IQR = Q3 - Q1 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is another way of thinking about it: Q1 is the median of the lower half of the data (all values less than the median), and Q3 is the median of the upper half of the data (all values greater than the median). Similar to the median, the numbers Q1 and Q3 may or may not be actual numbers from the data set. In many cases, these values are calculated using technology.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection The Five-Number Summary and Boxplots

    The first and third quartiles are part of a set of values called the five-number summary. The five-number summary includes the minimum, Q1, median, Q3, and the maximum.
    🔗
    A quick way to graphically display the 5-number summary is with a boxplot, also known as a box-and-whisker plot. Each region of a boxplot contains approximately the same number of data values, allowing us to visualize the spread for each region.
    🔗

    Example 3.6.4.

    There is growing concern about the amount of electronic products disposed of in landfills. These discarded products are collectively referred to as “e-waste”. Not only does e-waste occupy scarce landfill space, but it often contains toxic substances. For example, e-waste consisting of cathode ray tubes (CRTs) from computer monitors and televisions contains lead and other heavy metals that can leach into groundwater.
    🔗
    In a University of Florida hazardous waste study, 36 models of CRTs were tested for the presence of leachable lead.
     26 
    State University System of Florida, Florida Center For Solid And Hazardous Waste Management, Characterization of Lead Leachability from Cathode Ray Tubes Using the Toxicity Characteristic Leaching Procedure, by Timothy G. Townsend et al., Report #99-5 (1999).
    The results are given in the table below.
    🔗
    From this data, we can calculate the following 5-number summary:
    🔗
    • Minimum: \(1\) mg/L
      🔗
    • Q1: \(\frac{1.6 + 2.2}{2} = 1.9\) mg/L
      🔗
    • Median: \(\frac{9.1 + 9.4}{2} = 9.25\) mg/L
      🔗
    • Q3: \(\frac{26.6 + 33.1}{2} = 29.85\) mg/L
      🔗
    • Maximum: \(85.6\) mg/L
      🔗
    🔗
    Note that the IQR is \(29.85 - 1.9 = 27.95\) mg/L.
    🔗
    These values can be used to construct the boxplot below. The boxplot consists of a box drawn from Q1 to Q3, with a vertical line through the box at the median. From the left and right sides of the box, “whiskers” are drawn out to the minimum and maximum values. In the boxplot, it is easy to see the small spread of the lowest 25% of the data, and the large spread of the highest 25%. Thus, the data is right-skewed.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Standard Deviation

    The final measure of variation we will cover in this section is standard deviation. The sample standard deviation measures the average distance of all data values from the mean.
    🔗

    Sample Standard Deviation.

    \begin{equation*} s = \sqrt{\frac{\sum(x-\bar{x})^2}{n-1}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(x:\) data value
    🔗
    \(\bar{x}:\) mean
    🔗
    \(n:\) number of data values
    🔗
    🔗
    There are a few important characteristics that should be kept in mind when finding and interpreting the standard deviation.
    🔗
    • The standard deviation is never negative. It will be zero if all the data values are equal, and it will become larger as the data spreads out.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The standard deviation has the same units as the original data.
      🔗
      🔗
    • The standard deviation, like the mean, can be highly influenced by outliers.
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection Z-Scores

    Have you ever heard the saying that you can’t compare apples and oranges? It turns out that you can - provided we standardize their measures first!
    🔗
    We introduced standard deviation as an overall measure of the spread of a data set away from its mean. We now use standard deviation to help describe the position of a single data point. The position is given by the z-score, which measures the number of standard deviations a data point lies above or below the mean. The convention in statistics is to say that a data value is unusual if it is more than 2 standard deviations from the mean, or in other words, if its Z-score is less than -2 or greater than +2.
    🔗

    Z-Score.

    \begin{equation*} z = \frac{x-\bar{x}}{s} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \(x:\) data value
    🔗
    \(\bar{x}:\) mean
    🔗
    \(s:\) standard deviation
    🔗
    🔗
    Z-scores may be used with any data, but they are most commonly used with unimodal or symmetric distributions, also known as normal distributions. Note that if a distribution is skewed, then the distribution of the z-scores will also be skewed.
    🔗

    Example 3.6.8.

    The tables below contain average July temperatures (°F) from 2017 to 2020 for three climate divisions in the U.S.
     29 
    https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/monitoring/climate-at-a-glance/statewide/time-series
    The tables also show the mean (30-year average) and standard deviation for each climate division. Find the z-score of each average temperature. Round each z-score to one decimal place.
    🔗
    1. Based on the climate division that you examined, in which year was the average temperature in July the most unusual? Explain.
      🔗
    2. What does it mean if a temperature anomaly (z-score) is positive? What does it mean if it is negative? What does it mean if it is zero?
      🔗
    3. Create a dotplot by combining the twelve z-scores from the three climate divisions.
      🔗
    4. What can we conclude about the July average temperatures in these three climate divisions from 2017 to 2020? Do these temperature anomalies suggest an increase in temperature in these regions compared to the respective 30-year averages?
      🔗
    🔗
    Solution.
    1. Answers will vary. In Central Ohio, the July 2017 average temperature of 73℉ is the most unusual because it lies approximately 0.92 standard deviations below the mean (z-score of -0.92).
      🔗
    2. If a temperature anomaly is positive, it is greater than the mean. If it is negative, it is less than the mean. If it is zero, it is equivalent to the mean.
      🔗
    3. 🔗
    4. The average July temperatures in these climate divisions from 2017 to 2020 are normally distributed, and none of them fall outside of two standard deviations from their respective means. Thus, there is no indication of an unusual temperature increase in any of these regions.
      🔗
